## Shared Script Craft Guide

Write like a teacher helping a learner think through the lesson, not like a textbook summary.

Use these principles:

- No audience or learning intent should produce bland narration. Audience changes assumed background, vocabulary, pacing, and examples; it should not remove warmth, curiosity, or conversational clarity.
- Start from a real problem, question, misconception, decision, artifact, or case that makes the objective matter.
- Give the lesson a loose arc: setup, obstacle, useful idea, worked moment, and payoff. Do not force every topic into the same rhetorical template.
- Prefer concrete examples before abstraction when the audience and subject allow it. Return to an example only when it helps the learner track the idea.
- Keep each narration beat focused on one main thought. Split beats that combine a new concept, a formal term, a consequence, and a future implication.
- Keep narration beats speakable: usually 20-35 words, never over 45 words.
- Vary the voice. Do not make every beat a tidy two-sentence block in the same word range. Mix explanation with questions, contrasts, predictions, "notice what changed" moments, and short emphatic beats.
- Motivate formal terms, notation, procedures, frameworks, or rules by the problem they solve or the mistake they prevent.
- Put exact formulas, code, tables, quotations, labels, and long notation in visuals. Narration should say what the formal detail means and why it matters.
- Do not put raw LaTeX, markdown math delimiters, code syntax, or long symbolic expressions in narration. Use plain spoken forms, then let the visual carry the exact notation.
- Avoid making the voice read coordinate pairs, formulas, or algebra chains as long literal strings. Say the meaning first, then use a short spoken label if needed. For example, prefer "the point at zero comma one swaps places with one comma zero" over a rapid list of coordinates.
- Use TTS-friendly wording. Prefer short, common words when they mean the same thing: "math researchers" over "mathematicians", "connection" over "relationship", "natural log" over repeatedly saying "natural logarithm", and "log button" over spelling out "L-O-G" after the term is established.
- Build in breathing room with punctuation and beat shape. Use occasional short beats, questions, and sentence fragments where a human teacher would pause. Do not write every beat as one continuous explanation.
- When narration points to a visual detail, match the visual exactly: signs, directions, positions, labels, variable names, before/after states, and left/right/top/bottom references.
- When explaining a result, name the actual cause shown in the visual or worked step. Do not invent a shortcut explanation that contradicts the formula, diagram, evidence, or example on screen.
- Keep advanced material rigorous without compressing too many abstractions into one sentence.
- In step-by-step reasoning, let the learner participate: ask what would happen, point out what changed, or contrast the tempting move with the better move.
- Avoid generic academic filler such as "this brings us to," "it is important to note," "this establishes," "now that we have," and inflated verbs like "utilize."
- Avoid jokes, hype, and decorative anecdotes unless they directly clarify the objective.
- End with a real lesson conclusion, not an abrupt stop or a point-by-point recap. Do not open the ending with "let us summarize", "by establishing this framework", "you are now equipped", or a list of everything covered. First synthesize the lesson's durable idea in plain language, then name the learner's new capability, then gesture toward a useful question or practice step. Keep the ending course-extension-safe: do not say the learner has finished the course, reached the final lecture, or is on lesson N of M. A checklist visual is fine only if the narration has already given the ending a human sense of closure.

Register contrast:

BAD: "Zero-based indexing is a numbering convention in which the first element of an array is assigned index zero."

GOOD: "If the first box is already at the start, how far do you move to reach it? Zero steps. That is the idea behind index zero."

The bad version is accurate but flat. The good version creates a small reason to listen, then attaches the term to an intuition.
